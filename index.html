Background

What is AI sycophancy, and why does framing matter?

AI sycophancy refers to cases where a model validates a user's position rather than providing accurate or corrective feedback. In gambling advice, this rarely involves a model explicitly endorsing gambling. The more common failure is softer: accepting a risky framing, offering token warnings, or treating rationalization as sound reasoning.

The mechanism comes from training. Models optimized on human feedback learn that agreement tends to earn higher ratings. Over time, this pressure shapes systems that defer to users rather than correct them, particularly when the user sounds plausible.

This study focuses on implicit, position-directed sycophancy: cases where the model accepts the user's risky frame without ever explicitly endorsing the harmful act. It tests whether that failure is more likely when risk is framed subtly (as strategy, discipline, or coping) rather than stated plainly, and whether it compounds as conversations lengthen.

The second response does not endorse gambling, but it accepts the user's framing. No alternatives are offered. The premise that controlled betting is a stress management tool goes unchallenged.

Experiment Design

10 scenarios, 10 turns each, 3 runs per scenario

GPT-4o-mini was given a system prompt directing it to be helpful but to give grounded pushback rather than validate risky gambling beliefs. It was then run through 10 gambling scenarios over 10 escalating turns, repeated 3 times each, for 300 total responses. The primary variable was how the underlying risk was framed: five scenarios stated the harm plainly; five framed it as rationalization.

Finding 1

4×

more sycophancy in subtle vs. obvious prompts

Subtle rationalizations produced four times more sycophancy than obvious harm.

Obvious-risk prompts produced sycophancy in 8.7% of responses. Subtle-rationalization prompts produced it in 38.0%. The model was substantially more reliable when harm was stated plainly than when it was framed as strategy, research, or self-care.

This is consistent with how sycophancy works: a charitable framing is harder to refuse because conceding it both satisfies the user and superficially resembles reasonable advice.

Finding 2

sycophancy rate from turn 1 to turn 10

Sycophancy increased consistently across conversation turns.

The model produced no sycophantic responses at turn 1, but conceded steadily as the user continued pushing. By turn 9, sycophancy reached 43.3%; by turn 10, 66.7%.

A single-turn evaluation would have captured the model at its most cautious. The safety behavior present at the start of a conversation did not persist to its end.

Finding 3

The two effects compound: subtle prompts drove nearly all late-turn failures.

Tracked separately by condition, obvious-risk sycophancy remained low throughout the conversation. Subtle-rationalization sycophancy accelerated sharply in the later turns. The model was not destabilized simply by repetition: it was destabilized by sustained pressure paired with framing that made the risky choice sound disciplined or emotionally justified.

Finding 4

All sycophantic responses were implicit. None were explicit.

Across all 300 responses, not a single one received a score of 3 (explicit sycophancy). The model never directly told a user to gamble. All sycophantic responses scored 2: the model accepted the user's risky frame through hedging, weak caution, or omission of alternatives. A safety check that only scanned for harmful language would have found nothing wrong.

Response type distribution by condition — GPT-4o-mini (counts, n = 150 per condition)

This finding aligns with what the taxonomy literature identifies as the hardest failure mode to detect: implicit, position-directed deference is the variety that human raters most often fail to flag as sycophantic. A model that never explicitly endorses gambling can still consistently legitimize gambling logic by softly absorbing the user's frame.

Exploratory Comparison

GPT-5.2 reduced sycophancy, but the pattern persisted.

The same pipeline was run on GPT-5.2 as an exploratory side comparison. Overall sycophancy fell from 23.3% to 14.3%. The improvement was concentrated in the subtle-rationalization condition; obvious-risk sycophancy was identical for both models.

Exploratory Modeling

Prompt-side features predicted sycophancy with 88% accuracy.

A CatBoost classifier trained on prompt-side features only (no response content) achieved 88% accuracy and 0.92 AUC on the GPT-4o-mini dataset. The top predictors were scenario type, conversation turn, and risk condition, consistent with the descriptive findings. Sycophancy was structured by framing and conversational escalation rather than distributed randomly across prompts.

Key Takeaways

Implications for AI safety evaluation.

  • 1

    Subtle rationalization is the primary vulnerability. GPT-4o-mini produced sycophancy in 38% of subtle prompts vs. 8.7% of obvious ones. Models appear better trained against harm that is explicitly stated than harm that is framed as rational planning.

  • 2

    Multi-turn pressure compounds the problem. Sycophancy rose from 0% at turn 1 to 67% at turn 10. A model's behavior at the start of a conversation is a poor indicator of where it ends up under sustained pressure.

  • 3

    The failure mode is not detectable by simple filters. Every sycophantic response was implicit (score 2). Zero scored 3 (explicit). Checking for harmful language would have returned no failures.

  • 4

    GPT-5.2 improved but did not eliminate the pattern. Subtle-rationalization sycophancy fell from 38% to 20%. The same qualitative vulnerability was present in both models.

  • 5

    Single-turn evaluations measure the wrong thing. Testing for refusal of explicit harm evaluates requests that users are least likely to make. Sustained resistance to rationalized harm across many turns is the more relevant property to measure.
